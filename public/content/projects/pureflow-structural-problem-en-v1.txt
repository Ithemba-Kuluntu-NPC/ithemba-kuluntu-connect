# SECTION 02: REPORTED ACCESS VS FUNCTIONAL ACCESS
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

section_02.anchor:
reported-vs-functional-access

section_02.small_heading:
The hidden gap

section_02.main_heading:
A tap on paper is not water in a cup

section_02.copy:
Official water-access data often records whether a household or community is linked to a reported water source. That matters, but it does not always show whether water is actually available, safe, close, reliable or usable today.

A communal standpipe can be counted as infrastructure even when no water comes out. A rainwater tank can be counted as storage even when the water inside is untreated. A household can be marked as having access even when women and children still walk to collect water from unsafe sources.

PureFlow Amanzi focuses on functional access. Functional access means the water is available when needed, close enough to use, safe enough to drink, understood by the household, and not dependent on hours of unpaid labour.

section_02.pull_quote:
Infrastructure can exist while safe water does not.

# SECTION 04: THE TRUE SOURCE
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

section_04.small_heading:
The true source

section_04.main_heading:
When taps run dry, families still need water

section_04.copy:
When municipal infrastructure fails, households do not stop needing water. They adapt.

In many rural villages, families collect water from rivers, streams, springs, open collection points, shared tanks and household containers. These sources are often unprotected. They may be exposed to livestock, environmental runoff, dirty containers, human handling and unsafe storage.

The water may look clean. But clear water is not the same as safe water.

section_04.supporting_copy:
Globally, WHO reports that at least 1.7 billion people use a drinking-water source contaminated with faeces. WHO also identifies microbial contamination from faeces as the greatest risk to drinking-water safety, and notes that contaminated drinking water can transmit diseases including diarrhoea, cholera, dysentery, typhoid and polio.

# SECTION 05: LOCAL FIELD REALITY
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

section_05.small_heading:
Field reality

section_05.main_heading:
Pondoland: where infrastructure on paper does not match life on the ground

section_05.copy:
In Cwebeni and surrounding rural Eastern Cape villages where iThemba Kuluntu works, communal standpipes line roads and settlement areas. On paper, this can look like access. In reality, many of these taps have not provided reliable water for years. In some parts of the village, community members report that no water has flowed from the communal taps for more than a decade.

Households survive through whatever source is available: streams, springs, rivers, shared tanks, roof runoff, buckets and containers. Some families have JoJo tanks. Many do not. And even when a tank exists, stored rainwater is not automatically safe drinking water.

This is why PureFlow Amanzi does not begin with assumptions from a spreadsheet. It begins with what families are actually drinking.

# SECTION 06: RAINWATER AND STORAGE
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

section_06.small_heading:
Storage is not safety

section_06.main_heading:
A water tank does not automatically mean safe drinking water

section_06.copy:
Rainwater collection can help families survive, but it is not a fair or universal solution. It depends on things poor households often do not have: a strong roof, proper gutters, a clean catchment surface, a tank, secure fittings, maintenance knowledge and money for repairs.

Even when a tank is present, water can become contaminated before it enters the tank and again when it is collected, poured or stored. Roofs collect dust, leaves, insects, bird droppings and animal waste. Tanks may be open, cracked or rarely cleaned. Water is often handled with buckets and containers used for many household tasks.

A tank can store water. It does not guarantee safe water.

# SECTION 07: POVERTY AND WATER CHOICES
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

section_07.small_heading:
Poverty and access

section_07.main_heading:
The poorest households have the fewest safe water choices

section_07.copy:
When municipal water fails, money decides what options a household has.

A household with resources can install gutters, buy a tank, repair plumbing, pay for water delivery, buy bottled water, use gas or electricity to boil water, or transport water from elsewhere.

A poor rural household often cannot. The available options are usually the hardest and least safe: walking to a stream, carrying water uphill, waiting for a tanker, sharing from a neighbour, drinking stored rainwater, using open containers, or boiling water over firewood when wood is available.

Unsafe water is therefore not only an infrastructure issue. It is a poverty issue. Families with the least money are pushed toward the highest-risk water sources, then carry the consequences through illness, lost time, school disruption and household stress.

# SECTION 08: WOMEN AND CHILDREN
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

section_08.small_heading:
Gendered burden

section_08.main_heading:
Unsafe water turns into unpaid labour

section_08.copy:
When water is not available at home, someone must find it, carry it, store it, make it usable and care for the household when it causes illness.

In rural communities, this work falls heavily on women, grandmothers, teenage girls and children. A 20-litre container weighs about 20 kilograms before the weight of the bucket itself. Carrying water over rough paths and long distances places strain on the back, neck, shoulders, hips and knees. For children, it also takes time away from rest, play, schoolwork and childhood.

Water collection is rarely one task. It is part of a daily chain: walk, wait, fill, carry, store, collect firewood, make fire, heat water, cool it, pour it, clean containers, repeat.

section_08.supporting_copy:
Global reporting on the 2026 UN World Water Development Report warned that women collect water in more than 70% of rural households without mains water across the developing world, and that women and girls collectively spend 250 million hours every day collecting water.

# SECTION 09: JUST BOIL IT
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

section_09.small_heading:
The cost of boiling

section_09.main_heading:
“Just boil it” shifts the burden onto the household

section_09.copy:
Boiling water can reduce biological risk when done correctly. But telling rural households to “just boil water” ignores the labour behind boiling.

Someone must collect firewood, carry it home, make a fire, wait for the water to heat, cool it safely and store it safely. If the water is later poured into dirty containers or touched with unsafe utensils, it can be contaminated again.

For households without reliable electricity or gas, boiling is not a quick safety step. It is another daily burden. It takes time, fuel, physical effort and attention, and it often falls on the same women and girls already responsible for water collection, cooking, childcare and household care.

PureFlow Amanzi exists because households need a safer option that does not rely on electricity, chemicals, daily firewood collection or boiling.

# SECTION 10: HEALTH AND LEARNING
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

section_10.small_heading:
Health and learning

section_10.main_heading:
When children get sick, the whole household pays

section_10.copy:
Unsafe water does not only cause illness. It changes the rhythm of the household.

When a child gets sick, someone must stay home. Someone must find taxi money. Someone must travel to a clinic, wait, explain, collect medicine if available, and return home. In rural areas, this can mean long distances, poor transport access, overcrowded facilities and inconsistent care.

For children, repeated illness can mean missed school, low energy, poor concentration and weaker learning. For caregivers, it means lost time, stress and extra costs. For households already living close to the edge, one illness can affect food money, transport money, school needs and daily survival.

section_10.supporting_copy:
WHO identifies diarrhoeal disease as the third leading cause of death in children aged 1 to 59 months. It estimates that diarrhoea kills around 443,832 children under five each year, and says a significant proportion of diarrhoeal disease can be prevented through safe drinking water, sanitation and hygiene.

section_11.small_heading:
Beyond the household

section_11.main_heading:
The crisis reaches schools and clinics too

section_11.copy:
The water crisis does not stop at the household gate. It reaches the places where children learn and where families seek care.

When a school has no reliable water, children cannot practise hygiene consistently and WASH education becomes harder to turn into daily behaviour. When a clinic runs dry, infection prevention, basic hygiene and dignified patient care are compromised.

This is why safe water must be understood as a public-health issue, an education issue and a community-resilience issue, not only a household convenience.

section_12.small_heading:
Not one broken tap

section_12.main_heading:
This is a system problem, not a household failure

section_12.copy:
The families drinking unsafe water are not failing. The system around them is failing.

A grandmother did not create the broken tap. A child did not choose to drink from a stream. A mother did not create the maintenance backlog, the missing water-quality testing, the dry standpipe, the lost treated water, or the municipal failure behind it.

The Department of Water and Sanitation has stated that, despite support to municipalities, water services continue to decline and fundamental reform is required to arrest and turn around the decline in municipal water and sanitation services.

# SECTION 13: PUREFLOW RESPONSE

section_13.small_heading:
The response

section_13.main_heading:
We do not wait for the infrastructure to fix itself

section_13.copy:
PureFlow Amanzi does not pretend that a household-level implementation model repairs South Africa's water infrastructure overnight. It does something immediate and practical: it helps protect families while the larger system remains unreliable.

By deploying gravity-fed, electricity-free filtration into homes, schools and early childhood centres, the programme secures the final line of defence: the household cup.

But this is not only a filter. PureFlow Amanzi combines filtration with WASH education, household onboarding, local women-led delivery, follow-up and field monitoring. It helps families understand that clear water is not always safe water, and that safe storage, handling, cleaning and continued use matter.

The goal is practical: reduce daily risk, reduce unpaid labour, protect children, support caregivers and build a layer of safety where formal infrastructure has failed to reach.

section_13.closing_line:
Safe water is not a convenience. It is the first layer of health, learning, dignity and opportunity.
