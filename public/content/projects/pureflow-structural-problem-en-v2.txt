section_02.anchor:
reported-vs-functional-access

section_02.small_heading:
The hidden gap

section_02.main_heading:
A tap on paper is not water in a cup

section_02.copy:
Official water-access data often records whether a household or community is linked to a reported water source. That matters, but it does not always show whether water is actually available, safe, close, reliable or usable today.

A communal standpipe can be counted as infrastructure even when no water comes out. A household can be marked as having access even when women and children still walk to collect water from unsafe sources.

PureFlow Amanzi focuses on functional access. Functional access means the water is available when needed, close enough to use, safe enough to drink, understood by the household, and not dependent on hours of unpaid labour.

section_02.pull_quote:
Infrastructure can exist while safe water does not.

section_04.small_heading:
The true source

section_04.main_heading:
When taps run dry, families still need water

section_04.copy:
When municipal infrastructure fails, households do not stop needing water. They adapt.

In many rural villages, families collect water from rivers, streams, springs, open collection points, shared sources and household containers. These sources are often unprotected. They may be exposed to livestock, environmental runoff, dirty containers, human handling and unsafe storage.

The water may look clean. But clear water is not the same as safe water.

section_04.supporting_copy:
Globally, WHO reports that at least 1.7 billion people use a drinking-water source contaminated with faeces. WHO also identifies microbial contamination from faeces as the greatest risk to drinking-water safety, and notes that contaminated drinking water can transmit diseases including diarrhoea, cholera, dysentery, typhoid and polio.

# SECTION 05: LOCAL FIELD REALITY
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

section_05.small_heading:
Field reality

section_05.main_heading:
Pondoland: where infrastructure on paper does not match life on the ground

section_05.copy:
In Cwebeni and surrounding rural Eastern Cape villages where iThemba Kuluntu works, communal standpipes line roads and settlement areas. On paper, this can look like access. In reality, many of these taps have not provided reliable water for years. In some parts of the village, community members report that no water has flowed from the communal taps for more than a decade.

Households survive through whatever source is available: streams, springs, rivers, shared water points, roof runoff, buckets and containers. Some families have water storage. Many do not. And stored water should never be treated as safe drinking water without proper filtration, treatment and safe handling.

This is why PureFlow Amanzi does not begin with assumptions from a spreadsheet. It begins with what families are actually drinking.

Local field observation

section_05.field_note_copy:

# SECTION 06: WOMEN, CHILDREN AND UNPAID LABOUR
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

section_06.small_heading:
Women carry the system

section_06.main_heading:
When water fails, women and children carry the burden

section_06.copy:
Water failure is never gender-neutral.

When safe water is not available at home, someone must make the system work anyway. In rural households, that work falls heavily on women, grandmothers, teenage girls and children.

They walk to the water source. They wait. They fill buckets and containers. They carry the weight home. They store the water. They decide whether it needs to be boiled. They collect firewood. They make the fire. They heat the water. They cool it. They pour it into household containers. They clean the containers. They care for children when the water makes them sick.

This is not one task. It is a chain of unpaid labour repeated again and again.

section_06.supporting_copy:
Global reporting on the 2026 UN World Water Development Report warned that women collect water in more than 70% of rural households without mains water across the developing world, and that women and girls collectively spend 250 million hours every day collecting water.

Add one respectful photo of women, grandmothers or girls carrying water, standing near containers, preparing water or supporting children.

section_06.body_burden_heading:
The body pays too

section_06.body_burden_copy:
A 20-litre container weighs around 20 kilograms, before the weight of the bucket itself. Carrying water over rough paths and long distances places repeated strain on the back, neck, shoulders, hips and knees. For children, it also takes time away from rest, play, schoolwork and childhood.

section_06.firewood_tax_heading:
The firewood tax

section_06.firewood_tax_copy:
Boiling water can reduce biological risk when done correctly. But telling rural households to “just boil water” ignores the work behind it. For homes without reliable electricity or gas, boiling means collecting wood, carrying it home, making a fire, heating water, cooling it, storing it safely and trying to prevent recontamination.

section_06.firewood_tax_note:
Boiling is not a simple safety step when the household already carries the cost of failed infrastructure.

# SECTION 07: POVERTY AND ACCESS
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

section_07.small_heading:
Poverty and access

section_07.main_heading:
The poorest households are left with the least safe options

section_07.copy:
When public water systems fail, poverty decides what a family can do next.

A household with money can buy water, pay for transport, repair broken pipes, install private systems, use electricity or gas to heat water, or find another paid solution. A poor rural household often cannot. When there is no money, the only options left are usually the hardest, slowest and least safe: walking to a stream, collecting from an open source, carrying water uphill, waiting for an unreliable tanker, sharing from a neighbour, using open containers, or boiling water over firewood when wood is available.

This is why unsafe water is not only an infrastructure issue. It is a poverty issue. The families with the fewest resources are pushed toward the highest-risk water sources, then carry the consequences through illness, lost time, school disruption and household stress.

section_07.supporting_copy:
Poverty turns water into a daily calculation: how far to walk, how much to carry, whether there is money for transport, whether there is wood to boil, whether the water looks clean enough, and whether the household can risk drinking it today.

section_07.pull_quote:
Poverty does not only reduce income. It reduces the number of safe choices a household has.

section_08.small_heading:
Health and learning

section_08.main_heading:
When children get sick, the whole household pays

section_08.copy:
Unsafe water does not only cause illness. It changes the rhythm of the household.

When a child gets sick, someone must stay home. Someone must find taxi money. Someone must travel to a clinic, wait, explain, collect medicine if available, and return home. In rural areas, this can mean long distances, poor transport access, overcrowded facilities and inconsistent care.

For children, repeated illness can mean missed school, low energy, poor concentration and weaker learning. For caregivers, it means lost time, stress and extra costs. For households already living close to the edge, one illness can affect food money, transport money, school needs and daily survival.

section_08.supporting_copy:
WHO identifies diarrhoeal disease as the third leading cause of death in children aged 1 to 59 months. It estimates that diarrhoea kills around 443,832 children under five each year, and says a significant proportion of diarrhoeal disease can be prevented through safe drinking water, sanitation and hygiene.

section_09.small_heading:
Beyond the household

section_09.main_heading:
The crisis reaches schools and ECD centres too

section_09.copy:
The water crisis does not stop at the household gate. It reaches the places where children learn, eat, play and receive care.

Some schools, ECD centres and clinics have no reliable running water at all. Others may have water on site, but that does not mean the water is safe for drinking. In many rural education settings, children and teenagers are still expected to drink from the same unsafe sources used at home: stored water, buckets, untreated tap water, streams or other unverified sources.

When learning spaces do not have safe drinking water, WASH education becomes harder to practise. Handwashing routines are interrupted. Children drink less, drink unsafe water, or bring unsafe water from home. For young children in ECD centres, this creates a daily health risk inside the very spaces meant to protect and prepare them.

Safe water in schools and ECD centres is not an extra. It is part of a safe learning environment.

section_09.stat_line:
WaterCAN has reported that 25% of state schools and 46% of clinics do not have running water, as cited by the Financial Times. But the field reality goes further: even where water is present, drinking-water safety is often not guaranteed.

section_10.small_heading:
The response

section_10.main_heading:
Safe water must reach the places where life happens

section_10.copy:
PureFlow Amanzi starts with a simple practical truth: people cannot wait for broken systems to become reliable before children can drink safer water.

The programme brings a locally developed, gravity-fed, non-electric filtration system into households, schools and early childhood centres. But the filter is only one part of the model. Each rollout is supported by local assembly, household onboarding, WASH education, safe-use training, follow-up and field monitoring.

This matters because unsafe water is not only a technical problem. It affects women’s time, children’s health, school readiness, household costs and community resilience.

PureFlow Amanzi turns safe water into a practical entry point for wider change: healthier homes, safer learning environments, local work opportunities, stronger rural support systems and a clearer evidence base for what communities are actually facing.

section_10.closing_line:
Safe water is the first layer. From there, health, learning, dignity and opportunity become possible.
